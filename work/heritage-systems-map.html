Overview

This Advanced HCI project looks at why cultural and natural heritage sites can remain vulnerable even when protection frameworks are already in place. Using systems thinking as a design research method, the project maps the tangled relationships between heritage sites, indigenous knowledge, climate pressure, governance structures, tourism, community involvement, and illicit trade, turning dense research into an interactive, explorable structure.

Research Challenge

How can HCI and systems thinking make the tangled pressures on heritage visible enough to act on, without oversimplifying the politics, culture, and ecology involved?

Tools & Skills

Built in Miro using systems mapping techniques including causal loop diagrams, feedback loops, and stakeholder modelling. The work drew on skills in systems thinking, visual communication, research synthesis, and designing for complexity, structuring messy, interdisciplinary content into a format that people could actually navigate and learn from.

Research Scope

I reviewed academic and policy literature on heritage destruction, Indigenous land rights, cultural preservation law, environmental vulnerability, and the illicit antiquities trade. Sources ranged from UNESCO frameworks and Indigenous knowledge scholarship to environmental heritage case studies, spanning built, natural, and intangible heritage contexts.

Methodology

The project started with a broad literature review, then moved into affinity mapping and thematic coding to identify clusters of pressure and protection across different heritage contexts. Those clusters were translated into a systems map that visually encodes causal relationships, feedback loops, and leverage points. I used colour coding and spatial layout to make the complexity readable, so that someone exploring the map could trace how one factor (like underfunding) cascades into others (like loss of knowledge or weakened enforcement).

Industry Relevance

The final output is a comprehensive, interactive systems map that shows how heritage vulnerability is shaped by intersecting forces rather than isolated threats. It works both as a research artefact and as a communication tool, making it easier to see where interventions might have the greatest impact.
