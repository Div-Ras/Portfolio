Master’s Thesis UX Research Media Architecture

From fragments to flow

A user-centred media architecture thesis exploring how fragmented urban spaces can feel more connected, memorable, and human.

This project became my way of bringing together architecture, UX research, and interaction design. Set around Kurilpa Bridge in Brisbane, it looks at how small moments of light, sound, and participation can shift a place from being just a route into something people notice, remember, and quietly share.

Overview

My thesis explores the space between media architecture and the human experience, specifically focusing on the Kurilpa Bridge in Brisbane. While the bridge serves as a vital physical link, the act of crossing it often feels impersonal and fleeting. I wanted to see if I could shift that feeling. Using a research through design approach, I moved from observing the rhythm of the bridge to crafting a prototype called Residues of Passage. It is a light and sound installation that gently gathers the traces of passersby, allowing their presence to linger in the space for those who follow.

Research Challenge

How can media architecture foster a sense of shared presence in transient public spaces without imposing interaction, creating visual noise, or disrupting the natural flow of the site?

Tools & Skills

To understand the bridge, I relied on a variety of methods including contextual inquiry, behavioural mapping, and long-form interviews. My technical toolkit was equally diverse, ranging from Qualtrics and Google Colab for data processing to web technologies and physical electronics for building the installation itself. Balancing these analytical and creative tasks required me to stay grounded in the site's unique character. I constantly asked myself if the interventions were serving the people who used the bridge or simply taking up space.

Research Scope

Over the course of a year, I immersed myself in the life of the Kurilpa Bridge. I observed how people moved through it at different times, noted the subtle social cues they exchanged, and talked to them about their experiences of the space. This deep dive provided the raw material for a series of design cycles, where each finding refined my understanding of how a small, deliberate installation could make a large, impersonal bridge feel a little more human.

Methodology

The research followed a two-stage process. In Stage 1, I focused on understanding the site through contextual inquiry, observation, and interviews. What became clear was that people do notice sensory qualities on the bridge (the light, the wind, the views, other people walking past) but only briefly, almost in passing. Stage 2 translated those insights into Residues of Passage, a glowing orb prototype that plays back anonymous audio traces left by previous visitors. I then ran a mixed-method evaluation with participants to explore how people approached the object, what made the interaction feel meaningful, and where the concept still needed work. One of the most valuable realisations was that treating urban space as a design material reframed the bridge entirely: it stopped being just a transit route and became a site of quiet, layered participation.

Industry Relevance

As cities pour investment into smart infrastructure and public-facing technology, there is a growing need to ask better questions about what that technology is actually for. This research explores what it looks like to design for ambient presence rather than active engagement, to use sensory feedback that respects the pace of a place, and to position technology as something that supports subtle human connection rather than becoming a destination in itself.
